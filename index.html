Watching a hair cell die, and some survive.

Cisplatin is a life-saving chemotherapy drug with a lasting cost: it destroys the sensory hair cells the ear relies on for hearing, and in mammals those cells never grow back. This site follows what happens inside hair cells after cisplatin exposure — from cell death itself down to the shape, motion, and membrane potential of their mitochondria — across a range of doses and recovery timepoints.

Everything here comes from the zebrafish lateral line, a small external sensory system that lets us image living hair cells directly.

More than half of children treated with cisplatin develop permanent, irreversible hearing loss.

Cisplatin is one of the most effective and widely used chemotherapy drugs, prescribed for cancers ranging from testicular to ovarian to pediatric brain tumors. Its side effect on hearing is well documented but poorly prevented: the drug is toxic to the sensory hair cells inside the inner ear, the cells that convert sound vibrations into the electrical signals the brain interprets as hearing.

Once those hair cells die, they do not regenerate in mammals. The hearing loss is permanent, and it disproportionately affects children, who then have to grow up and learn language with reduced hearing. Understanding exactly how cisplatin kills hair cells — and why some cells resist while others don't — is a first step toward protecting hearing during treatment rather than losing it.

Why a fish?

Zebrafish carry hair cells in an external sensory system called the lateral line, organized into small clusters called neuromasts. These hair cells are functionally and molecularly similar to the ones inside the human inner ear — but because they sit on the surface of the fish rather than buried in bone, they can be imaged live, dosed with drugs, and tracked over time in a way that would be impossible in a mammal.

That accessibility is what makes this dataset possible: the same hair cell, and the mitochondria inside it, can be followed through damage and, in some cases, recovery.
